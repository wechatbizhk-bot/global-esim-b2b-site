Market analysis

Is this market worth your investment?

Here is the evidence, with the source attached to every number. Travel eSIM revenue grew 85% in a single year and wholesale roaming revenue is forecast to double. What follows is what is genuinely established, what is a projection, and what is practitioner folklore you should treat with suspicion — including our own commercial interest in telling you the market is large.

Demand

The demand side: more travellers, and phones that can take an eSIM

Two curves have to cross for this market to work. People have to travel, and their handsets have to be capable. Both are moving in the same direction, and the second one has only just started.

Travel is at record volume

International tourist arrivals reached 1.52 billion in 2025, up 4% and roughly 60 million more than 2024. International tourism receipts came in at about USD 1.9 trillion, up 5%. This is not a recovery story any more; it is a record, and it is the base on which every connectivity forecast sits.

Source: UN Tourism — international tourist arrivals up 4% in 2025.

Growth is uneven by region, which matters more for a wholesale business than the global total does. Europe remains the largest destination region at 793 million arrivals (+4%), the Americas stand at 218 million (+1%), and Africa grew fastest at 81 million (+8%). Regional mix is what determines which wholesale rates you will actually be buying against.

Source: Xinhua — regional 2025 arrival figures.

Handset capability is the real constraint

Device adoption is the gating factor, and it is accelerating sharply. eSIM phones accounted for 37% of global smartphone sales in 2025 and are forecast to reach 48% in 2026. The installed base lags sales, which is precisely why the opportunity is still early: most capable handsets in the field have never had a travel profile activated on them.

Source: Counterpoint Research, reported November 2025.

The adoption numbers that matter

  • 605 million eSIMs shipped in 2025, up 18% year on year, with consumer eSIM profile downloads up 43% — downloads growing more than twice as fast as shipments is the sign of an installed base switching on (Trusted Connectivity Alliance).
  • eSIM connections go from 500 million in 2024 to 4.5 billion by 2030, with the activation rate on capable handsets forecast to reach 75% by 2030 — today's default is still a phone that can, and does not (Kaleido Intelligence).
  • 37% of smartphone sales in 2025, 48% forecast for 2026 (Counterpoint Research) — and in twelve markets, 100%, because there is no SIM tray left to put a card in.

Sources: Trusted Connectivity Alliance · Kaleido Intelligence · Counterpoint Research

Read the 43% carefully

Shipments up 18% while profile downloads rose 43% tells you the market is not being driven by more hardware. It is being driven by more people activating on hardware they already own — which is exactly the behaviour a distribution channel can influence, and the reason a retail brand can grow faster than the handset cycle.

Inflection

What changed in 2025 and 2026

Three things happened that were not in anyone's 2023 model. Together they moved eSIM from a niche product for frequent travellers to the default format in new handsets.

China turned eSIM on

In October 2025 MIIT approved a nationwide smartphone eSIM pilot. All three carriers launched eSIM service simultaneously, and Huawei, OPPO and vivo committed to eSIM models. The world's largest handset market moved in a single quarter.

Source: Counterpoint Research, reported November 2025.

Twelve markets lost the SIM tray

The iPhone 18 Pro generation is eSIM-only in twelve markets — the United States, Canada, Mexico, Guam, the US Virgin Islands, Japan, Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE. In those markets there is no physical alternative for a traveller to fall back on.

Source: MacObserver — the full list.

eSIM is now the default in new flagships

At 37% of global smartphone sales in 2025, rising to a forecast 48% in 2026, eSIM is already the standard format in flagship launches and the only format in twelve markets — and it is closing on half of all new phones sold worldwide.

Source: Counterpoint Research · see also Trusted Connectivity Alliance.

Why the China angle is the one to watch

These three developments interact. The China pilot switches on a domestic device base of enormous size, and every one of those new eSIM handsets is a device that can hold a travel profile. The same device base then leaves the country: outbound Chinese travel is one of the largest source markets in the world, and until now it has been served largely by roaming and by physical cards bought at the airport.

A supplier with a Guangzhou network core and direct China Mobile and China Unicom interconnect is structurally advantaged on exactly that traffic. It is not a marketing claim — it is the difference between buying mainland capacity at one remove and terminating it directly, and it shows up in the cost per gigabyte you are quoted for outbound-China and Greater Bay Area routes.

The timing is the point. The device base switches on inside China at the same moment that outbound demand returns to record levels. A channel that waits for the market to be obvious will be buying capacity after the wholesale rates have adjusted to it.

What we would do with this if we were you

Build the China and Greater Bay Area routes first, with a supplier that owns or directly interconnects mainland capacity, and treat them as the pilot volume. Bundle them with the destinations your customers most often combine with a China trip — Japan, South Korea, Thailand, Singapore.

Then expand the rate card outward on proven volume, rather than launching sixty destinations at a rate you have not tested and hoping the mix works out.

A market finding in its own right: nobody publishes a price

No major eSIM wholesaler publishes a public per-gigabyte rate card. Rates are disclosed only under NDA, after a qualification call, and usually after you have told them your volumes. That is not a small detail — it means you cannot comparison-shop this market from a website, and any supplier who refuses to put a rate card in writing is asking you to buy on trust.

We publish indicative regional rates on this site precisely because the alternative is a market where buyers cannot tell whether they are being quoted a fair price. Published rates are also why we can hold a rate for a committed volume in writing.

The arithmetic is the argument: the support, refund and processing lines are close to fixed per transaction, so they consume roughly two thirds of a USD 2.29 sale and all of a USD 1.50 one. This is why payment processing at roughly 3% plus USD 0.30 makes sub-USD 2 products structurally poor, and why the volume end of the market is a supply-chain game rather than a pricing game.

How to read these numbers honestly

The wholesale cost ranges, the provisioning fee, the support rate and the refund rate in this section are practitioner-reported and indicative. They come from public write-ups by people operating eSIM marketplaces, not from a carrier rate card, and they will differ from your own numbers.

Two operational facts from the same source deserve emphasis because they are strategy, not trivia: wholesale rates can change on 30 days' notice, and a supplier with no notice clause can move your margin after you have published a price to your customers. Ask for the notice period in writing before you build a campaign on a rate.

A formal margin model requires your own rate card. We will build one with you — but we will not pretend a public article is one.

Implications

What this means for you

Four practical conclusions, each tied to a number above rather than to an opinion.

The demand is already there — you are not creating a category

1.52 billion international arrivals in 2025 and 37% of new phones sold being eSIM-capable mean the traveller and the device already exist. Your problem is distribution and pricing, not market education. That is a much better problem to have.

The growth is in wholesale and enterprise, not only consumer retail

Wholesale roaming goes from USD 9 bn to USD 20 bn by 2028, and enterprise eSIM management grows at 31.3% CAGR to USD 8.63 bn by 2030. Corporate and wholesale channels grow faster and churn slower than consumer app sales — and they are less exposed to a single brand outspending you.

Your structural advantage is on the China side

The October 2025 MIIT eSIM pilot switches on the device base at the same time as record outbound travel. A supplier with an owned Guangzhou core and direct China Mobile and China Unicom interconnect prices that traffic from its own cost base. Start your pilot on the routes where that advantage is real.

Margin discipline decides whether this works, not market size

A ~20% support rate at 15 minutes per ticket and a 5% refund rate cost more than the data on a small bundle. Build for activation reliability, self-service installation and a rate card with a notice period — or a growing topline will not produce a growing contribution.

One competitive warning worth repeating

Airalo raised USD 220 m at a valuation above USD 1 bn and stated it will use the capital to provide connectivity to companies. The best-known consumer brand in this market has declared its intention to compete for your channel. If you are choosing a supplier, ask what happens to your margin when your supplier's largest competitor becomes your supplier's largest competitor and a direct B2B vendor.

A note on evidence quality

Not everything on this page is equally solid, and it would be dishonest to present it as if it were.

Market sizing, device adoption and regulatory figures come from research houses, industry bodies and public authorities — Juniper Research, Counterpoint Research, Kaleido Intelligence, Trusted Connectivity Alliance, The Business Research Company, UN Tourism and national statistics agencies. They are citable, but the forward-looking ones are still projections: a 2030 figure is a model, not a result, and this sector's forecasts have been revised in both directions before now.

Wholesale rate ranges, profile provisioning fees, support-cost estimates, refund rates and activation-failure observations are practitioner-reported. They come from public write-ups by people who operate eSIM marketplaces. They describe real experience, but they are self-reported, unpublished, un-audited and they vary enormously by route, volume and supplier. Treat every one of them as indicative, and replace them with your own rate card before you build a business case on them.

On activation failure rates specifically: we searched for an independent, third-party study quantifying eSIM activation failure as a percentage and did not find one. None is quoted on this page. The industry consensus that activation problems are the dominant support cost is widely repeated but not, as far as we can establish, independently measured — so we describe it as a structural risk and a design priority, not as a statistic.

We would rather you trusted the parts of this page that are sourced than be impressed by a total that is not.
